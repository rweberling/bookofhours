Sciurus carolinensis

Meriwether Lewis, John Burroughs, Ralph Waldo Emerson

Headnote

My brother called them “joeys” and “jojos” as a child, for reasons that escape me. Maybe he was thinking of young marsupials or mispronouncing the word “chipmunk.” At any rate, squirrels were one of the first forms of wildlife detectable through our family’s windows, in our backyard, or at the playgrounds we frequented. Like many suburban households, our closest neighbors were likely not people but squirrels, who raise their young in tree hollows or in leafy nests called dreys, breeding twice annually: in late winter and again in summer. Indeed, these quintessential critters have a persistent presence: trees squirrels are active year-round, contributing to the bustling energy of spring and summer but also enlivening the sparse quiet of fall and winter. The creatures can be so ubiquitous that we overlook them, unless the young ones in our midst remind us how strangely hilarious they are. Or unless we consult the many literary works that feature them as companions, conspirators, omens, psychological antagonists, or other spectral friends.

However plain and obvious they seem now, grey squirrels were actually imported intentionally into cities as a form of harmless entertainment beginning in 1847, when they were introduced into Philadelphia’s Franklin Square (then Boston and other eastern cities, followed by Victorian Britain—which eventually regretted it). Before this shift, squirrels were considered serious agricultural pests for the damage they cause to wheat, corn, and other crops. No joke! They were hunted by the tens and hundreds of thousands, with local governments across the Midwest paying a few cents per squirrel scalp (or allowing residents to pay their taxes with squirrels). One 1807 law in Ohio even required all military-age males to kill 100 squirrels per year and deliver their hides to the local tax collector—or face a penalty. Squirrels were a common source of meat, and their furs were also used widely, both functionally and decoratively, in various clothing and accessories. Yet keen observers of nature also noticed the important ecological role that squirrels play as planters and cultivators of the great North American forests.

The eastern grey squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis) belongs to the primary tree-squirrel genus, Sciurus, which gives the rodent its common name (via the Old French escurel) and is taken from the ancient Greek skiouros (read by some to mean “shadow-tail,” as a description of how the animal seems to sit in the shade of its own tail). The squirrel genus is part of the broader squirrel family (Sciuridae), which includes tree, ground, and flying species, as well as several related animals: chipmunks, prairie dogs, groundhogs, and marmots—oh, my! North America is home to 60-odd species of squirrels, spread across many genera and branches of this family.

Despite their name, grey squirrels are subtly multi-colored, with banded gradations of color, white-tipped hairs, brownish face and leg tones, pale bellies, and silver-fringed tails. They can also be black or white, due to genetic variations within the species or introgression from fox squirrels (Sciurus niger). In fact, an unusually high proportion of otherwise-rare black squirrels has been a fun point of consistency between where I currently live in Massachusetts and my previous haunts in Western Michigan. One other thing worth knowing: because they can turn their hind feet backwards, grey squirrels are one of the few mammals that can descend a tree trunk headfirst. Talk about spunk. Their faces plunge toward the ground thousands of times each fall, as they plant thousands of acorns and other nuts in their caches, sorting red acorns from white acorns, deactivating some nut embryos to ensure they are still edible in the months to come, and choosing just the right depth in the ground for overwintering. In the process of their labors, squirrels reseed the forest, spread nutrients and spores, and aerate and mix the topsoil, unintentionally perpetuating the woods around us.

Set out about sunrise, passed Sunfish creek 1 Mile &c &c entered the long reach, so called from the ohio runing in strait direction for 18 miles in this reach there are 5 Islands from three to 2 miles in length each—observed a number of squirrels swiming the Ohio and universally passing from the W. to the East shore they appear to be making to the south; perhaps it may be mast or food which they are in serch of but I should rather suppose that it is climate which is their object as I find no difference in the quantity of mast on both sides of this river it being abundant on both except the beach nut which appears extreemly scarce this season, the walnuts and Hickory nuts the usual food of the squirrell appears in great abundance on either side of the river—I made my dog take as many each day as I had occation for, they wer fat and I thought them when fryed a pleasent food— many of these squirrils wer black, they swim very light on the water and make pretty good speed—

  1. The elegant creature, so cleanly in its habits, so graceful in its carriage, so nimble and daring in its movements, excites feelings of admiration akin to those awakened by the birds and the fairer forms of nature. His passage through the trees is almost a flight. Indeed, the flying squirrel has little or no advantage over him, and in speed and nimbleness cannot compare with him at all. If he miss his footing and fall, he is sure to catch on the next branch; if the connection be broken, he leaps recklessly for the nearest spray or limb, and secures his hold, even if it be by the aid of his teeth.

    His career of frolic and festivity begins in the fall, after the birds have left us and the holiday spirit of nature has commenced to subside. How much his presence adds to the pleasure of a saunter in the still October woods. You step lightly across the threshold of the forest, and sit down upon the first log or rock to await the signals. It is so still that the ear suddenly seems to have acquired new powers, and there is no movement to confuse the eye. Presently you hear the rustling of a branch, and see it sway or spring as the squirrel leaps from or to it; or else you hear a disturbance in the dry leaves, and mark one running upon the ground. He has probably seen the intruder, and, not liking his stealthy movements, desires to avoid a nearer acquaintance. Now he mounts a stump to see if the way is clear, then pauses a moment at the foot of a tree to take his bearings, his tail as he skims along undulating behind him, and adding to the easy grace and dignity of his movements. Or else you are first advised of his proximity by the dropping of a false nut, or the fragments of the shucks rattling upon the leaves. Or, again, after contemplating you a while unobserved, and making up his mind that you are not dangerous, he strikes an attitude on a branch, and commences to quack and bark, with an accompanying movement of his tail. Late in the afternoon, when the same stillness reigns, the same scenes are repeated. There is a black variety, quite rare, but mating freely with the gray, from which it seems to be distinguished only in color.

John Burroughs. From “Squirrels.” Squirrels and Other Fur-Bearers. 1875. Boston and New York: Houghton, Mifflin and Company, 1900. 7-8.¶
