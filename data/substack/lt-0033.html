Headnote

We interrupt this fall’s journey through the northeast woodlands with a reflection on the threshold that is the first frost (prima pruina, if it were a Latin binomial).

Is it death or birth that defines the earthly conditions of the human? Hannah Arendt famously argued that, while the biggest questions of philosophy tend to be oriented around the fact of our mortality, it is in fact natality—biologically, the birth of new people; and politically, the possibility of new beginnings—that grounds and enables our individual and collective life. As she writes in her book, The Human Condition, “Only the full experience of this capacity [i.e., natality] can bestow upon human affairs faith and hope” (247). In a looser sense, natality is a feature of the natural and plant worlds as well as human life and history. We can see it even in the first frost, whether the slow dying of a gentle freeze or the harsh punctuation of necrosis.

Yes, the first frost (prima pruina) marks a turning point, for many plants, between anthesis and senescence—that is, between the developmental phase in which a flower is fully open, functional, and capable of pollination; and the protective process through which plants dismantle themselves in order to preserve their vital nutrients and core architecture. But it’s more of a gradient than a line: a dip in temperatures may blacken tender basil leaves or dahlias, but hardy kale and asters will persist for a while longer. Here in the Boston area, first frost typically falls sometime in the days after Halloween—a few weeks later than in surrounding areas of New England, due to the combined influences of coastal climate moderation and urban heat islands. Yet the changes introduced by the first frost involve life and nuance as much as sharp loss.

Let me count some of the ways:

  1. Samuel Taylor Coleridge was only half correct when he observed that “The Frost performs its secret ministry, / Unhelped by any wind.” For the frost does accept the help of a bacterium, Pseudomonas syringae, which lives on leaf surfaces and “seeds” ice a few degrees below freezing, when plain water would stay liquid. Some frost damage, in other words, is partly microbial: a living thing is part of the freeze.

  2. Frost is also a sweetener. Hardy crops such as kale and parsnips convert stored starch to sugar as temperatures fall, lowering the freezing point of their sap and protecting their cells, so that the plant’s defense becomes our flavor. Likewise, fruits such as persimmon and medlar benefit from some degree of bletting, as they linger in that amorphous transition between ripening and decay.

  3. As the nights cool, the decay of a leaf reveals some of its true colors. The yellows and oranges of autumn foliage, at least, are carotenoids present all summer, unmasked as the chlorophyll disintegrates and its nutrients are reclaimed by the tree (the red anthocyanins, however, are made fresh in the fall).

  4. Some plants make frost of their own. This is not merely the bloom of powdery pruina you may have noticed on grapes or blueberries (or other living things), itself named after the frost. Instead, various species commonly called “frostweed” draw water up through their stems, which split in the cold and release the water as curling ribbons of ice. Neither frost nor flower, these “freak[y]” blooms are the plants’ own sap, produced as they shut down for winter.

  5. As a necessary phase in the life cycle of some plants, winter also can serve as an instruction. Milkweed and several native coneflowers cannot germinate until they undergo what gardeners call cold stratification: weeks in cold, moist conditions, which reset the chemical and hormonal clocks for germination. For such seeds, natality requires the cold.

  6. Some plants simply decline to stop. Several native and immigrant species bloom in autumn or winter; for example, witch hazel’s ribbon-like petals curl in the cold and unroll when it warms, while hellebores and snowdrops open in late winter, sometimes through snow. For them, anthesis is not interrupted by the cold but persists through it.

  7. In the concept of “frostmas,” which has spread on social media over the past few years, primarily among small-scale flower growers and farmers, the death of the plants can bring a rush of beauty and much-needed rest—and new economic questions and challenges, as sources of income necessarily shift.
