Headnote

Rounding out my quartet of summer berries (fraudulent, actual, or imaginatively so), we arrive now at the blueberry—my apologies to the sweet strawberry, that rosaceous receptacle, which will have to wait for another summer.

Apart from a handful of medieval and early modern instances, most human food crops were domesticated thousands of years ago, during the globally distributed neolithic, or so-called first agricultural, revolution. However, blueberries stand alongside kiwifruits, Hass avocados, macadamia nuts, and wild rice (manoomin) as important crops that only began to be cultivated scientifically and commercially in the twentieth century—although, as is often the case, evidence of indigenous harvesting, management, and multifarious use extends back thousands of years.

The blueberry’s modern, agronomic story goes something like this: in 1911, botanist Frederick Coville teamed up with cranberry farmer Elizabeth White (heir to the Whitesbog company town infamously documented by Lewis Hine). They found that blueberries require acidic, peat-and-sand soil—à la North America’s prehistoric blueberry “barrens”—rather than neutral, rich soil, to thrive. They also ran a contest encouraging locals near White’s home in New Jersey to submit the largest wild blueberries they could forage, and then crossbred these selections to produce larger, sweeter, and more uniform berries. While the collaboration went on to develop hundreds of these hybrid varieties, it was in fact an especially fantastic wild specimen, submitted during the initial contest, that became the first commercial crop harvested in 1916. New cultivars (including more warm-tolerant ones), mechanization, and national expansion followed over the rest of the century. Then, sometime in the 1990s, several studies identifying blueberries’ high antioxidant and anthocyanin levels launched this newly minted “superfood” into a global, year-round, rapidly expanding, multi-billion-dollar industry expected to produce 3.2 million metric tons of berries by 2028. Though globalization, as usual, can have devastating local consequences.

The recentness of this history makes the process of domestication much more visible than for other cultured plants. It reveals how closely implicated modern agriculture can be in the developmental interests of the state. Indeed, Coville’s employer was the U.S. Department of Agriculture; and his Experiments in Blueberry Culture, the foundational research document in the history of blueberry domestication, was produced by the Bureau of Plant Industry (RIP in 1953) and distributed by the “government printing office.” A more critical and intimate story of the blueberry, as Alix Beeston notes below, might touch on the morals it is chosen to convey in children’s literature and on how the indulgent succulence of a certified-organic commodity comes to embody both the insulating protections of capital and the various guilts that society displaces onto (young) women’s appetites. A more reparative story of blueberries might be found in the abundance experienced by Wabanaki caretakers through their recovery of ritual, tradition, kinship, and self-determination in the blueberry barrens of eastern Maine—or in celebratory poems by Robert Frost, Mary Szybist, Mary Oliver, or James Lasdun.

Proper blueberries belong to the Cyanococcus section (Greek kyanos, “dark blue” + kokkos, “berry”) of the genus Vaccinium, which also includes cranberries, bilberries, lingonberries, some (but not all) huckleberries, and the less-known because not-tasty-to-humans farkleberry/sparkleberry. The majority of commercial blueberry cultivation involves cultivars of the primary “highbush” species (Vaccinium corymbosum), although a non-trivial proportion of the market consists of several “wild” lowbush species (e.g., Vaccinium angustifolium). These wild varieties emerged in the northeastern corner of the continent some 10,000 years ago, when glaciers receded and left behind fields of thin, acidic topsoil. You can visit them today and look out over ancient tangles of stems, roots, and berries, spreading and multiplying via underground clones. And yet each barren contains a multitude of subtle variations in color, taste, shape, and genetic makeup. Makes you wonder what we mean when we call something “barren.”

Readings

  1. Whitesbog Blueberries are a real money-maker for the commercial grower.

    Note that the photographed branch of these remarkable berries is reproduced here actual size. No picture, however, can convey any idea of the delicious flavor, which while keeping the delightful tang of wild blueberries, is sweeter and smoother. Whitesbog Blueberries are practically seedless; they are firm and keep well. They are exceptionally heavy yielders, with all the requirements for a marketable and highly profitable fruit, commanding highest prices wherever sold.

    Read inside the whole story of this remarkable berry. Learn how carefully the different varieties are propagated in the Whitesbog Nurseries. How the commercial plantations which are now in bearing are realizing a yearly return of more than $1000 an acre. How land now unprofitable for any other crop may be well adapted to blueberry cultivation.

Joseph J. White, Inc. Whitesbog Blueberries (1925). U.S. Department of Agriculture, National Agriculture Library.

  1. Eat one blueberry and you’ll eat them all. In Robert McCloskey’s Blueberries for Sal, an award-winning illustrated children’s book published in 1948, plucky Sal sets out with her mother to pick fruit on Blueberry Hill. ‘We will take our berries home and can them,’ her mother says. ‘Then we will have food for the winter.’ Sal can’t help herself. The blueberries barely hit the bottom of her tin pail – ‘kuplink, kuplank, kuplunk!’ – before they’re scooped into her mouth, a cheeky smile on her lips.

    In Roald Dahl’s Charlie and the Chocolate Factory (1964), Willy Wonka forbids Miss Violet Beauregarde from chewing his magic gum, a new and untested invention. But she, a self-proclaimed chewing-gum champion, grabs a stick and shoves it into her mouth. The gum contains a three-course dinner, with blueberry pie for dessert, and it’s in this final course that things go wrong. From nose to cheeks, hands to arms and legs, a ‘brilliant, purplish-blue, the colour of blueberry juice’ spreads like a dye over Violet’s skin and hair. ‘Violet, you’re turning violet, Violet!’ shrieks her mother, as her daughter becomes ‘an enormous round blue ball – a gigantic blueberry, in fact – and all that remained of Violet Beauregarde herself was a tiny pair of legs and a tiny pair of arms sticking out of the great round fruit and little head on top’. In the 1971 film adaptation, Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory, Violet’s red belt makes a satisfying pop as it gives under the pressure of her expanding stomach. Ballooning with berry juice, consumed by that which she consumes, she’s rolled off by the Oompa Loompas as they sing their smug, moralising song: ‘It’s repulsive, revolting, and wrong, chewing all day long – the way that a cow does!’

    Violet, that rude little cow! So disdainful, in her marathon mastication, of the manners and prohibitions of adults, with the perfectly devastating contempt of an almost-teenaged girl. And so unseemly in her bald-faced competitiveness, her elbow-jostling self-interest.

    By design – Dahl’s design, his un-prim but still reactionary morality tale – the prideful and indecorous Violet is far less likeable than the cheerful Sal. Yet both of these girls are rebellious blueberry-eaters. They refuse to defer to the adults or to delay the gratification of their desires. Blueberries, in these stories, are associated with young women’s illicit acts of consumption, illicit in both timing and excess. The girls eat when they shouldn’t and they eat too much. Once they start, in different ways, they can’t stop.

Alix Beeston. “Fingers Stained With Fruit and Ink.” Rev. of Ellena Savage, Blueberries: Essays Concerning Understanding (2020). The Sydney Review of Books (June 15, 2020).
