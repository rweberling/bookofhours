Headnote

At the risk of this becoming an extraterrestrial blog, I am continuing last week’s astronomical theme as we exit the peak of the Perseids meteor shower. Although it offers no liquid relief to the summer heat, this shower is a long debris trail with an equally long tale to tell.

Our dear little planet is peppered by Perseids around the same time every year, although the timing fluctuates slightly due to calendar mechanics and the wobbling of the Earth’s axis, gradually shifting later in the year at a rate of approximately 1.5 days per century. And the shower is replenished every 133 years, when the Swift-Tuttle comet again enters our inner solar system from the distant Kuiper Belt and crosses Earth’s orbit on the way to its perihelion (closest point to the sun). As it passes through our neighborhood, the 16-mile-wide comet sheds some of its frozen gases, rock and mineral debris, and carbon compounds, leaving a renewed debris trail for our planet to spin through.1 The comet’s last visit to the sun was in December 1992, and it won’t return again until July 2126 (nearly 100 years from today). In the meantime, though, in 2028, we will pass through an especially rich “filament” of dust shed by the comet in 1479, which is expected to amplify the annual shower into an outright meteor storm.

The first recorded observation of the Perseids shower was made in China nearly 2,000 years ago, in the extensive manuscript annals of the Han Dynasty (202 BCE to 220 CE), and was preserved for centuries through the region’s dedicated manuscript culture. This ancient observation was first compiled and translated for European audiences in 1841, as part of a French text presented below for your decoding pleasure. Yes, this took me a while to track down and verify, even with the help of a chatbot. But it was, I submit, worth it to encounter that ancient scribe’s evocative turns of phrase, transmitted to us across continents, millennia, and media forms.

Humans didn’t really figure out how meteor showers and shooting stars worked, including the regularity of their timing, until the nineteenth century. Prior to that, some Catholic cultures in Europe referred to the Perseids shower as the “tears of St. Lawrence”; the feast day of this martyred saint—who is supposed to have told the people burning him to death, “This side is cooked enough; turn me over and eat!,” thereby securing his spot as the patron saint of both cooks and comedians—falls on August 10th. It wasn’t until 1838 that New Haven bookseller (and future Yale librarian) Edward C. Herrick more fully connected the dots across several years and, eventually, much of recorded history. This was the era that recognized meteors as more than just transient atmospheric phenomena, unrequiring of further study; for example, amateur British astronomer W. F. Denning first documented, in detail, how the Perseids shower gradually moves across the sky as Earth passes through the comet debris.

Now, with those millennia of accumulated attention and eventual documentation behind us, humans in the twenty-first century can watch a state-sponsored live-stream of Perseids on Youtube and poetically chronicle “this vertiginous awareness / mocking gravity on our speck of now.” The infrastructure of our attention runs deep.
