Headnote

Few trees in New England, except perhaps the marvelous paper birch (Betula papyrifera), have quite the visual appeal of the shagbark hickory. The distinction is in the name. A fully shagged-out trunk embodies something between an elegant cascade and a strewn-out mess—a poise we might all hope to achieve, if we are so lucky.

Hickory trees found their name in English by shortening the Algonquin or Powhatan word, pawcohiccora, which refers to the flour or milk made from crushing hickory nuts dry or in water. Their name thus embodies not only this culinary-cultural past but, as writers like Elspeth Hay (in her recent book, Feed Us With Trees) and food foresters around the country are demonstrating, a potential agricultural, social, and ecological future. After all, much of the North American continent, as well as regional and peasant cultures around the world, were once sustained by edible landscapes of fire-shaped forests that produced an abundance of nuts, other nourishing substances, and useful objects. Today, researchers and agroforesters across the Northeast and Midwest are making a healthy, delicious culinary oil from shagbark’s cousin, the Yellowbud, or bitternut, hickory (Carya cordiformis). This all made me curious about the forests that surround us here in New England.

Massachusetts, as the state likes to brag, is the third-densest state in the U.S. but also the 11th-most-forested. Thus, forests comprise a major variety of what are referred to politely, by the local government, as “the natural communities of Massachusetts”: these include formations such as “acidic rock cliff community” and “cultural grassland.” The hickory genus (Carya) contains 19 recognized species, 12 native to North American and seven to Southeast Asia). Hickories are close companions of oaks, including the northern red oak I wrote about last week. Whereas oaks are in the beech family (Fagaceae), hickories are in the walnut family (Juglandaceae); but both are grouped in the higher-level order of beeches (Fagales), all distinguished by the presence of a hard cupule (hull or husk) that partially or completely encloses their fruit.

Together, these two trees define the oak-hickory forest type that represents approximately one-third of the state’s forested land: that’s more than 1,000,000 acres! Such forests are home to some of my favorite plants, including witch-hazel and the pink lady’s slipper orchid. Oak-hickory forest is one of the more diverse types of oak communities, falling near the middle of a spectrum between dry, acidic scrub oak scrublands (where they mix with wild blueberries and other heath species) and riparian, flood-prone oak wetlands (where they mix with ash, willow, or sweetgum) Though companions, oaks and hickories do compete, with fast-growing oaks racing to the canopy while hickories root deep, establish a small stand, and wait in the under-canopy, perhaps for centuries, until an opening appears—a poise we might all hope to achieve, if we are so lucky.
