Headnote

While fall may be the season of the woodlands, the transition into winter can shift plant and animal activity toward the lower, tighter structure of all those water-adjacent landforms: fens, marshlands, bogs, mires and quagmires, swamps, sloughs, and swales—not to mention bayous, pocosins, and the many other regional variations of wetlands. If you’ve never stopped to wonder how these terms relate and differ, you’re not alone. Here’s what I’ve learned through a couple late nights spent down the (water-logged) rabbit hole.

The short version is that wetlands are sorted by water source and plant growth. A marsh is ruled by soft-stemmed plants (grasses, sedges, rushes) and can contain freshwater (palustrine), saltwater (estuarine), or a mix (brackish). A swamp is a wetland with trees or shrubs. Bogs and fens, both also called “mires,” gradually build up layers of peat; they differ primarily in where their water comes from. A bog, fed only by rain, turns acidic and nutrient-poor, while a fen is fed by mineral-rich groundwater, which makes it less acidic and allows it to support a wider variety of species. Fens are also transitional wetlands: as their peat thickens, their surface can lift above the reach of groundwater, eventually transforming them into rain-fed bogs. The other wetlands are mostly regional dialect. The pocosin of the Carolinas takes its name from an Algonquian word usually glossed as “swamp on a hill,” for evergreen shrub bogs raised on their own peat. Swales, sloughs, and bayous refer to the low shape of the ground more than what specifically grows there. Fen is one of the oldest wetlands words: Old English fenn (“marsh, mud, dirt”) reaches back through Proto-Germanic fanja- (“swamp, marsh”) to the Proto-Indo-European root pen- (“swamp”).

I’ve written previously about how the easternmost edge of Massachusetts shares ancient bedrock with England and Wales. While this shared subterranean layer has little to do with either region’s contemporary topography, the two coincidentally share several features that make them ripe for wetlands. Their extensive patches of fens and salt marshes often lie in glacially sculpted, low-lying coastal basins. These basins are lined with impermeable clay and dotted with islands of harder bedrock or glacial deposits, which together trap freshwater runoff and the sea as it rose after the glaciers melted.

In 1630, the same year that my current hometown was founded as “Newtowne,” the English began in earnest to drain the vast fenlands region sprawling for approximately 1,500 square miles north of the University of Cambridge. This region is where Hereward the Wake and other English militants had staged their heroic resistance against William the Conquerer—a strategy replayed across the Atlantic when escaped slaves, indigenous Americans, and other groups gathered for shelter or insurgency. The great drainage projects north of Cambridge had begun more than a millennium prior, with Roman ditch-digging projects, but it achieved infrastructural scale when a group of aristocrats hired a Dutch engineer, Cornelius Vermuyden, to cut two straight channels, the Old and New Bedford Rivers, to help drain the Ouse River to the sea. Fen dwellers, who were losing their common grazing, fishing, and reed-cutting rights to this “improvement,” fought back as the Fen Tigers, tearing down sluices and setting reed beds on fire. To no avail.

In Massachusetts, the tidal salt marshes and freshwater swamps surrounding the former Newtowne, now known as Cambridge, were buried rather than drained. The “Great Marsh” that once cut East Cambridge off from the rest of town was gone by the late nineteenth century, filled in with whatever debris the small city was accumulating, including ash and cinders. Something like a third of the city now stands on filled wetland. Across the Charles River, meanwhile, Frederick Law Olmsted began building a new “fen” from scratch. His Back Bay Fens, begun in 1879, was in fact designed as a restored salt marsh that would be flushed by the tides twice a day (it doubled as a sewage and flood basin). However, when the Charles River Dam arrived in 1910, it shut out the tides, making Olmsted’s salt marsh into a freshwater one: closer to its name, though by the definitions above still not quite a fen.

In both Cambridges, the majority of these ancient ecosystems were altered or erased. Less than one percent of the original English fens survives undrained, and the Boston area has lost about four-fifths of its salt marshes. “And, muse and marvel where we may, / Gain mars the landscape every day,” as John Clare writes in his poem “The Fens.”

  1. A certain sadness is pardonable to one who watches the destruction of a grand natural phenomenon, even though its destruction bring blessings to the human race. Reason and conscience tell us, that it is right and good that the Great Fen should have become, instead of a waste and howling wilderness, a garden of the Lord, where

    ‘All the land in flowery squares,
    Beneath a broad and equal-blowing wind,
    Smells of the coming summer.’

    And yet the fancy may linger, without blame, over the shining meres, the golden reed-beds, the countless water-fowl, the strange and gaudy insects, the wild nature, the mystery, the majesty--for mystery and majesty there were--which haunted the deep fens for many a hundred years. Little thinks the Scotsman, whirled down by the Great Northern Railway from Peterborough to Huntingdon, what a grand place, even twenty years ago, was that Holme and Whittlesea, which is now but a black, unsightly, steaming flat, from which the meres and reed-beds of the old world are gone, while the corn and roots of the new world have not as yet taken their place.

    But grand enough it was, that black ugly place, when backed by Caistor Hanglands and Holme Wood, and the patches of the primaeval forest; while dark-green alders, and pale-green reeds, stretched for miles round the broad lagoon, where the coot clanked, and the bittern boomed, and the sedge-bird, not content with its own sweet song, mocked the notes of all the birds around; while high overhead hung, motionless, hawk beyond hawk, buzzard beyond buzzard, kite beyond kite, as far as eye could see.

Charles Kingsley. “The Fens.” Prose Idylls: New and Old. London Macmillan and Co., Limited, 1900. 89-90.
