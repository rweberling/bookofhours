Headnote

To untangle the messy world of squashes and melons, I will be spending the next three weeks zooming in on some of our favorite summer delectations, moving from the broad gourd family (Cucurbitaceae) to the distinctive melon-and-cucumber genus (Cucumis) and, finally, to the wholly separate watermelon species (Citrullus lanatus).

The cucurbits are a messy family despite being domesticated approximately 11,000 years ago across Asia, Africa, and the Americas (ask them about their transatlantic and trans-Siberian journeys). Cucurbitaceae contains approximately 100 genera and nearly 1,000 species distributed around the world and coming in many surprising variations. For example, there are the trombone-shaped heirlooms and the spongy luffa/loofah genus; there is even a squirting cucumber (Ecballium elaterium) that builds up immense pressure before launching its seeds up to 12 meters through the air. While a variety of gourds and melons were first cultivated in Asia and Africa, all “true” squashes—those in the Cucurbita genus—originated in the Americas, where they have been nurtured as part of the three sisters and carefully preserved by seed keepers for thousands of years.

Amidst this diversity, many of our most familiar crops are drawn from just one species, Cucurbita pepo, home to both summer and winter squashes in all their multiplicity. On the summer side: zucchinis and marrows, straight necks and crooknecks and roundeds, scallops and pattypans, cousa and tatume heirlooms, striped and speckled and hybrid gradients, and yellows and greens and grays and goldens! The abundance can be overwhelming, as my fridge experienced this past month—and as Piercy and Regan document in the readings below. And then in winter: acorns, delicatas, spaghettis, and sugar and field pumpkins. However, autumn and winter also bring notable members of Cucurbita moschata (butternut, specialty pumpkins, etc.) and Cucurbita maxima (Hubbard, kabocha, and friends).

Poison also lingers in the mix. Like the butterfly weed, cucurbits produce bitter, toxic steroids as a defense mechanism. These appropriately named cucurbitacins have been bred almost entirely out of domesticated squashes, although cross-pollination with a wild or ornamental relative can reawaken the ancestral pathway, producing a fruit capable of inducing toxic squash syndrome (nausea and hair loss, oh my!). Even environmental stressors, such as drought or excessive heat, can lead to elevated toxin levels in a squash, though this is more rare and typically more mild in effect. Thankfully, poisonous cucurbits are so bitter that humans rarely manage to eat them in dangerous quantities. Keep that in mind if a squash ever puckers your lips.

Errata

In last week’s post regarding the AD 36 meteor logs from Édouard Biot’s 1841 catalog, the poetic phrase “a vast migrating crowd” was highlighted in relation to the Perseids. While Biot calculated this event as June 27, a landmark 1958 recalculation by astronomers Susumu Imoto and Ichiro Hasegawa revealed a translation error in the months. The “migrating crowd” event actually occurred on August 30 and was likely a minor stream outburst such as the Aurigids. The true, historic first record of the Perseids belongs to the earlier entry on that page, which Biot misdated to February 9, but actually occurred on July 17.

Readings

  1. With a suave reptilian glitter
    you bask among your raspy

    fronds sudden and huge as

    alligators. You give and give

    too much, like summer days

    limp with heat, thunderstorms

    bursting their bags on our heads,

    as we salt and freeze and pickle

    for the too little to come.

Marge Piercy. “Attack of the Squash People.” 1978. Circles on the Water: Selected Poems of Marge Piercy. New York, New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 1982.

  1. 2

    Courgettes are a type of squash: their flowers can be male or female, but both wear skirts of yellow taffeta. An arrangement of various small squashes can make an excellent alternative to flowers as a centrepiece for the table at an autumn dinner-party.

    3

    You could also try working pumpkin into your menu, or emphasising the inversion by serving courgette flowers stuffed with soft goat’s cheese & coated in tempura. Have you ever considered taking a cookery class as a way of making new friends?

    4

    The streaks of yellow-green on the bottle-green colour-field of the courgettes recall the ‘zip-line’ paintings of Barnett Newman. In contrast, the turban-squash is surely the product of Joan Mitchell. The butternut represents Rothko at his most luminous.

    5

    I steam the ribbons of courgette with salt & black pepper; what comes to mind is vermicelli, or the hagfish’s classification as vermiform. Yes, I realise there’s more to life than perfectly describing the perfect squash, but I can’t imagine what that might be.

Padraig Regan. From “Notes on Various Squashes.” The Poetry Review (Autumn 2017).
