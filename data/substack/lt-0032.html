Headnote

Is it a bush? Is it a tree? Is it a poison? Is it lemonade? In the lower gaps of the forest, or in its damaged peripheries, the sumac thrives. Like the pawpaw, it often spreads through root suckers, producing a grove of clones in meadow edges, riverbanks, or cleared land. Its conical flowers and then spike-like berry clusters develop over the summer, and its leaves erupt into vibrant colors in the early fall. The plant’s leaves and bark contain tannins that were used for centuries, around the world, to tan light and flexible leathers and as a mordant to dye fabrics. In North America, the berries were steeped to produce a brilliant pink, slightly acidic tea comparable to lemonade. And after falling out of favor in European cuisines, the tart, peppery flavor profile persisted in the Middle East and is now poised for a global comeback.

The sumac genus (Rhus) contains somewhere between 35 and 100+ species, depending on who’s counting—47 is an officially recognized number—with roughly 20 species native to North America. Sumac plants can be found in temperate regions around the globe, although the genus may have originated in western North America (the oldest known fossils come from Washington State). The sumac genus lends its common name to the broader sumac family (Anacardiaceae), perhaps more commonly known as the cashew family, which contains approximately 80 genera and 850+ species, including those that grow mangos, cashews, pistachios, and marulas. So-called poison sumac (Toxicodendron vernix), which I for some reason vaguely feared as a child, is in a separate genus within that larger family, along with poisons oak and ivy. These species all produce the highly inflammatory resin, urushiol, known for occasionally causing “mango mouth” and for harming the people—many of them women, children, and forced laborers—who process cashews. But of course, because botany makes so much sense, the true sumacs for which the plant family is known do not produce urushiol.

Both the common and Latin names have interesting etymologies. The descriptor “staghorn” is mostly transparent. It was adopted by English settlers in North America to distinguish the plant’s fuzzy, forking branches from its smoother brethren, such as the aptly named smooth sumac (Rhus glabra). This association with horns was formalized in the 1750s, when Carl Linnaeus and his student, Ericus Torner, identified the plant as Ramis hirtis uti typhi cervini (“with branches hairy like antlers in velvet"). The word “sumac” has an even longer history, descending from Arabic (summaq) and Classical Syriac/Aramaic (summāqā) words for “dark red,” which were absorbed into Medieval Latin and Old French around the 13th century. Though the word first referred specifically to the chopped berries of the plants, its meaning later expanded to include other parts of the plant used for tanning leather, dyeing textiles, or as a medicinal astringent. Finally, by the mid-1540s, it was applied to the living plants themselves.

As for the Latin, the name of the “Rhus” genus comes from the classical Latin and Greek names for sumac plants, notably Sicilian or tanner’s sumac (Rhus coriaria), known for its leather-tanning and dyeing properties. The typhina descriptor for staghorn sumac also was supplied by Linnaeus and Torner, in their comparison of the plant to the hairy antlers of a male deer (typhi cervini)—although, that description itself may itself echo the cattail (typha), another plant named for its fuzzy spike. Some might see this as a muddle of language and taxonomy. But that muddle embodies the fuzzy perceptions and tangled analogies that have built our basic picture of the world around us.
